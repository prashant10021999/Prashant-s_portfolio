Teaching Philosophy

How I teach physics

Myself Prashant Sir, a JEE and Physics Olympiad coach. When a student gives me an answer, my next question is often “Why do you think that?”

That’s what I take from Socrates. He asked people to explain their ideas, then asked another question to see if their explanation held up. I do the same in class. You make a prediction, tell me why you believe it, and then we change one thing and see what happens. Over time, I want you to be able to ask those questions yourself.

A stone leaves your hand. Why does it keep going up?

Imagine lifting a stone on your open palm. Your hand is moving upward and getting faster. While the stone is still moving up, you move your hand out of the way without giving it an extra push.

Does the stone immediately move down?

A student might say, “Yes, because gravity pulls it down.” I’d ask, “When your hand moves away, what disappears?”

“The upward push from my hand.”

“Good. And which way was the stone moving at that instant?”

“Up.”

The push from your hand disappears immediately. The stone still has its upward velocity at the moment you let go. Once the stone leaves your hand, gravity accelerates it downward. So it keeps going up for a short time, slows down, and then falls.

Here’s the next question I’d ask in class. At the very top, the stone’s velocity is zero for an instant. Is its acceleration zero too? Gravity is still pulling it down, so its acceleration is still downward.

First-principles thinking means following cause and effect

The net force tells you the acceleration right now. The acceleration tells you how the velocity is changing. To know which way the stone is moving just after release, you also need to know the velocity it already had. Knowing Fnet = ma and a = dv/dt is the starting point. The skill is knowing what each equation tells you about the stone at that moment.

That’s why first-principles thinking matters for JEE Advanced and Physics Olympiads. The exam can give you a situation you’ve never seen before. You can still begin by asking “What was the object doing? What changed? What does that change affect?” We practise those questions in class so you can work out an answer from the basic laws on your own.

Practise physics in daily life

Mechanics · When the bus brakes

You feel yourself lurch forward. In the road’s frame, the bus slows while your body tends to keep moving at its earlier speed. Ask what force actually slows you down. A seat belt does that job in a car, and spreading the slowdown over more time reduces the average force for the same change in momentum.

When a mechanics problem feels crowded, pick one body and draw its forces. That first sketch is progress even if the rest of the solution takes time.

Thermodynamics · Which cup stays warm?

Start with two identical covered cups holding the same amount of warm water at the same temperature. Wrap one in a cloth sleeve and record both temperatures at equal time intervals. Which cools more slowly? Insulation design in buildings and transport starts with this same question about how quickly energy leaves a system.

If the readings surprise you, check your setup and repeat the comparison. A careful second attempt is part of doing science well.

Resonance · Find the rhythm of a swing

Small pushes can build a large swing when you time them with the swing’s natural rhythm. Try explaining why timing matters as much as the size of each push. Engineers ask a related question when they identify vibration frequencies to avoid or damp in bridges and machines.

When the wave equation feels abstract, sketch several cycles and mark where energy enters. A good picture can show you where to start.

Electrodynamics · What powers a bicycle light?

In a bicycle dynamo, motion changes the magnetic flux through a coil and induces a voltage. At the same wheel speed, supplying current to a lamp takes extra mechanical work. Lenz’s law tells you why the induced current opposes the change that produced it. The same energy accounting matters when engineers design generators and regenerative brakes.

Before you reach for a formula, trace where the energy comes from and where it goes. That habit will help you check the direction of an induced effect.

Debates that changed engineering

Was heat a fluid?

Many scientists once thought heat behaved like a conserved fluid called caloric. Joule used falling weights to turn paddles in water and measured its temperature rise. His results helped establish a quantitative link between work and heating. Engineers use that link today to track energy in motors, cooling systems, and machines that generate heat through friction.

My question for you is this. What measurement would help you tell two explanations apart?

See Joule’s apparatus

AC or DC for the power grid?

In the historical War of the Currents, AC had a practical advantage because transformers made it easy to raise and lower the voltage. Today’s engineers also use high-voltage DC for some long-distance links. The choice depends on the route, the equipment, and the losses the system can tolerate.

When you compare two solutions, ask which constraints matter. That’s how an exam habit becomes an engineering decision that can make electricity delivery more efficient.

M.Tech. thesis · IIT Jodhpur · 2025

What polarised light reveals about materials

Mueller Matrix for Nano-Sized Objects Manufactured Under Laser-Based Additive Process

My thesis examined how layered films, atmospheric particles and an electrochemically etched Al-7075 surface change the polarisation of light. I used Mueller matrices, which connect the state of the incoming light to the state measured after it passes through or reflects from a sample.

The work combined thin-film modelling, simulations of atmospheric light scattering and laboratory polarimetry. I learned to treat the optical setup and the simulation as two parts of the same question. What does the model predict, and what can the instrument actually measure?

Soleil–Babinet compensator

A sliding birefringent wedge lets you dial in a phase delay between two components of light. That makes the device useful for calibrating polarisation measurements and compensating for unwanted retardance in an optical system.

My contribution. I developed a library to calculate the compensator’s Mueller matrix. Modelling how a mechanical adjustment changes the optical signal helped me see why wedge travel, axis alignment and wavelength calibration matter when you design an instrument.

Monte Carlo for light scattering

I wanted to know how a beam’s polarisation changes when the photons take many different paths. I simulated many scattering histories and combined their contributions to estimate the outgoing light. Each path can look different, so the result depends on how well the sample of paths represents the scattering process.

Why the method interested me. The same idea appears in financial-market risk analysis. You can run many plausible return paths to see how widely outcomes vary. In both problems, the useful questions are what assumptions produced the distribution and how sensitive the result is to changing them.

Heliostat solar concentrator

I designed a heliostat-based solar concentrator in 3DOptix. The design question was simple to state and harder to solve. How do you keep reflected sunlight on a fixed receiver while the Sun keeps moving?

What I learned about design. The law of reflection tells you where to aim. A working design also has to account for how a small pointing error shifts the spot and how much light may miss the receiver. That made alignment a measurable design constraint.

Laser additive manufacturing

A laser builds a metal part by melting material along a programmed path. The path also determines where heat goes and how the metal cools. Those changes can affect the internal structure even when the outside of the part looks right.

How it changed my thinking. I learned to work backwards from a feature I wanted to explain. Choose a process variable, predict what it should change, and decide how to tell whether the prediction holds. My optical characterisation work gave me a way to connect that reasoning to measurements.
